Policy brief

Who gets to decide what counts as progress?

I start from the view that safe water, livable housing and dependable access to supplies are collective needs. I look at budgets and jurisdiction because they shape who gets the resources to meet those needs, who sets the terms, and who has to wait.

What the Manitoba snapshot shows

The federal water tracker records advisory and project milestones independently.

Housing conditions and winter-road access add context for infrastructure planning.

What I would change

  1. Follow the water after construction. Report when people can use the water again, what it takes to keep the system running, and whether the Nation has the resources to do that work. First Nations should direct what gets shared.
  2. Budget for the costs people already live with. Use routes, freight windows and costs confirmed by First Nations when planning construction and funding ongoing operations.
  3. Give First Nations control over how need is defined. Start with each Nation’s service priorities and rules for sharing its records. Track commitments, actual spending and remaining needs separately so the public can see where the money went.

How I approach the evidence

I approach this project from an anti-colonial political economy perspective. First Nations should set their own infrastructure priorities, and governments should answer for the commitments they make. I use public records to ask what governments funded, what they counted as progress and what we can verify about the service people actually received.

That is why I track project status and water advisories separately. I use the Census for a provincial housing comparison and Manitoba’s reports for named winter routes. Each source answers a different question.

Coverage and joins

ISC band numbers link the Manitoba region records to the official First Nations Location points and the reviewed treaty and winter-road lookups. The points represent administrative locations and sometimes approximate locations. Treaty affiliations follow ISC’s text description of signatories, including Treaty 5 adhesions.

Water advisories and infrastructure projects

ISC’s map covers long-term advisories on federally supported public systems on reserve. Advisory counts refer to systems and episodes in the dated export. Other systems, private wells and short-term advisories fall outside that coverage. Current local notices govern water use.

Project stages come from the same ISC export. Published amounts in that export carry multi-community funding notes and cannot be allocated to individual Nations from those records. The funding chart uses a separate ISC release with five named project amounts. A capital gap estimate requires compatible asset-condition, replacement-cost and spending data.

Winter roads and all-weather access

The winter-road layer identifies 18 First Nations linked to place names in Manitoba’s published northern and southern road reports. A route listing describes a seasonal route or crossing. A route can appear alongside other road access. ISC reports 17 Manitoba First Nations without all-weather road access in its regional overview. That provincial figure uses ISC’s classification.

Census definition and coverage

The housing measure uses the 2021 Census long-form table for people with a single First Nations identity in private households in Manitoba, across registration statuses and ages. The rate divides people in dwellings needing major repairs by people in the matching residence group. The gap is the difference in percentage points before rounding.

Estimates use rounded Census counts and omit incompletely enumerated reserves and settlements. A census subdivision and a Nation are different units. Community-level housing comparisons require a reviewed geographic crosswalk and compatible denominators.

First Nations data governance

For me, data governance starts with who has the power to decide how information is collected, used and shared. The First Nations principles of OCAP® describe ownership, control, access and possession. CARE emphasizes collective benefit, authority to control, responsibility and ethics. This project uses published government records. Nation approval and OCAP® adherence require their own process.

I would seek Nation-led terms for access, review, correction and stewardship before using community-held records or publishing more detailed assessments.
