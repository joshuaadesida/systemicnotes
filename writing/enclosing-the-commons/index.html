Enclosing the Commons Under the Banner of "Reconciliation":

Why Mark Carney’s Post–Indian Act Vision Threatens Treaty Sovereignty in Winnipeg

As the very first Anishnabbe Premier, Wabanakwut "Wab" Kinew has not disappointed in his tenure; so when he brought up abolishing the Indian Act at a First Ministers and First Nations meeting in Ottawa on October 26, 2026, our province took notice. Our central banker . . . I mean, 'Prime Minister' declared himself "very open" to abolishing the Indian Act. The support of the political and financial establishment was expected, but at the same time, I can't help but be cynical knowing our governments record. For over 150 years, the Indian Act has operated as a racist instrument of state control, imposed band-council governance, and patriarchal control over Indigenous identity. To call for its abolition sounds, on its face, like liberation, right?

I guess so; but from my radical anti-colonial and anti-capitalist political-economy perspective I hesitate, and I think you should too—or at least understand the implications. In my opinion, the crucial question is not whether the Indian Act ends—that decision should be left up to First Nations Peoples—but what property governance and mode of production come out of the ashes. Being the central . . . I mean, 'Prime Minister' he is, Carney by definition is no anti-colonial revolutionary. The ungodly mandate handed to Carney since Trump began pressing Canada will leave Canada in shambles years after he's gone. From the back-room dealings to gain a majority, to his proposed selling of public resources—like our fucking airports, to parasitic foreign capital—to him saying "oligopoly can lead to more competition"—you have to be dumb as rocks to believe this shit—when a technocrat of finance capital proposes moving "beyond" the Indian Act, Indigenous nations in Treaty 1 territory have every reason to suspect that what is being offered is not decolonization, just neoliberalism.

Primitive Accumulation as an Ongoing Structure

Let's all pull out our copy of Das Kapital please and thank you. Karl Marx described "primitive accumulation" as the violent historical process of divorcing producers from the land and converting communal territories into private, alienable capital. Yellowknives Dene political theorist Glen Sean Coulthard argues in Red Skin, White Masks: Rejecting the Colonial Politics of Recognition (2014), Where Marx saw primitive primitive accumulation as a completed historical stage; Coulthard shows that in settler-colonial states it never ends. Canada's dispossession is continuous, and when overt violence becomes politically unconscionable, the state substitutes the "colonial politics of recognition." The old "we hear you, we see you, we recognize you" HR lingo, while plotting on your down fall.

By granting symbolic recognition, limited self-administration, and market-friendly "economic reconciliation," the Canadian state induces Indigenous nations to identify with legal and economic forms that leave the underlying structure of capitalist land ownership intact. By abolishing the Indian Act under a neoliberal or finance-capitalist framework it fits hand and glove into what geographer David Harvey terms "accumulation by dispossession": when capital runs out of new frontiers, it turns inward to privatize, commodify, and financialize remaining public or collectively held assets.

The Urban Reserve Paradox at Naawi-Oodena

In critical theory and politics, "exhausting a dialectic" is a line of reasoning that pushes the contradictions to the absolute, till resolutions can be squeezed out of it. The most evident place we could see this play out on a local level is the Naawi-Oodena. It is a 65 hectare former Kapyong Barracks site repatriated to seven Treaty 1 First Nations and heralded by municipal and federal officials as Canada’s largest urban reserve. Its located near River Heights and Tuxedo—growing up I've always wondered what it was, I'm sure I wasn't the only one.

This land revels the precise delicacy of the Indian Act. For all of its colonial violence, the Act currently functions as a statutory barrier preventing reserve land from being alienated, seized by private creditors, or dissolved into fee-simple provincial real estate—incase you were confused why it has been left untouched. To develop repatriated land inside a capitalist city, First Nations are compelled to operate through municipal service agreements, commercial leaseholds, corporate development entities, and market financing.

As Michi Saagiig Nishnaabeg scholar Leanne Betasamosake Simpson writes in As We Have Always Done: Indigenous Freedom Through Radical Resistance (2017), settler capitalism always demands the transformation of the living, reciprocal relationships with land to what Coulthard calls grounded normativity i.e., into "resources" and "capital" ripe for exploitation and extraction. Under the capitalist logic, collective, inalienable Treaty land is viewed as "dead capital" and "underutilized"; which must be unlocked through 'equity deals', 'corporate partnerships', and 'market integration'. So when Carney pairs talk of dismantling the Indian Act with major-project investment frameworks and sovereign wealth vehicles, the political-economic objective becomes clear: convert sovereign nations from treaty holders into market stakeholders, corporate landlords, and minority equity partners in resource extraction and urban real estate.

Jurisdiction vs. Fiscal Warfare

Critical political economist Shiri Pasternak, in Grounded Authority: The Algonquins of Barriere Lake Against the State (2017), shows us how Canada systematically uses "fiscal warfare" and administrative control to hollow out Indigenous self-determination. So instead of recognizing Indigenous peoples' inherent autonomy over there land and life, Ottawa will likely seek to leverage funding formulas, statutory definitions, and provincial municipal boundaries to discipline communities into compliance with 'state-approved' governance models.

Sound familiar? To me it's a form of Neo-Colonial administration of Internal Colonial space.

Back in 1969, former Prime Minister Pierre Trudeau unveiled the 'White Paper'. It was aimed at abolishing the Indian Act, eliminating Indian status, and converting collective reserves into private fee-simple property. In 1971, the Manitoba Indian Brotherhood, now the Assembly of Manitoba Chiefs released a policy and position paper to the federal Minister of Indian Affairs (and future Prime Minister) Jean Chrétien. It was called Wahbung: Our Tomorrows. They stood ten toes and asserted that the unilateral termination of the Indian Act without honouring Treaty jurisdiction was simply assimilation disguised as formal equality.

Here we are, more than half a century later, the rhetoric has shifted from state assimilation to corporate "co-operation"; the structural mechanism remains unchanged: sever the Crown’s historical and fiduciary debt, absorb Indigenous territories into the circuits of private and foreign capital, and rebrand dispossession as "partnership".

What Genuine Abolition Requires

True treaties, were never real-estate zoning arrangements or corporate joint ventures, and any unjust encroachment the belligerent nation should be —in my opinion, met with militant force. They are nation-to-nation compacts predicated on shared coexistence, mutual obligation, and the enduring survival of Indigenous political and economic orders; even more so after a genocide had been committed.

To abolish the Indian Act, while leaving the capitalist property regime and the settler state's monopoly on jurisdiction untouched, is not to dismantle colonialism, but to strip away its only remaining restraint.

The Act is simultaneously one instrument of containment, and the statutory barrier against alienation; removing it under a finance-capitalist framework which has out top Liberal "very open" is unconscionable to me. If genuine abolition is the goal, it requires the restoration of inherent Indigenous jurisdiction, the unconditional return of land and wealth extracted from Treaty territories, and the absolute protection of collective land tenure from market alienation. Anything less is not the end of colonialism — it is its privatization.
